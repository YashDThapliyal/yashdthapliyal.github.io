Fooling a Trusted Monitor

Can an AI agent fool its safety checker by changing a few words in its own account of what happened?

Yash Thapliyal · September 2026 · 9 min read
AI ControlMonitoringAdversarialLayaSHADE-Arena

The idea

Imagine an AI assistant that can send email, move money, search files and call other software. We want a second system watching it. That safety checker reads the record of the assistant's work, including its reasoning, messages, tool requests and tool results, and raises an alarm if the assistant does something harmful. Researchers often call this checker a trusted monitor, and the record it reads a trace.

Jev and Laya are decision models that can be used for this kind of checking. They differ from chat models: instead of writing an open-ended response one word at a time, they choose among answers defined in advance and attach probabilities to those choices. Jev motivated this project, but its weights are closed. Laya's are public, so I could train it and look inside it.

Why I tested this

I kept seeing work on agents manipulating their monitors. Most monitors today are language models themselves: they read the agent's reasoning and messages and judge whether anything looks wrong. That makes them persuadable. An agent can explain away a harmful action, claim it was authorized, or wrap it in reassuring narration, and a model that reads and reasons about that text can be talked into approving it. In one study by Za et al., persuasive arguments alone got language-model monitors to approve harmful behavior up to 47% of the time.

Laya looked like it might avoid that. It doesn't follow instructions or reason out loud. It reads the whole trace once and picks from a fixed set of answers, so there's no conversation to steer and no chain of thought to argue with. My hypothesis was that a Laya monitor couldn't be manipulated by what the agent says, at least not the way a chat-model monitor can, and might make a better monitor for that reason.

The fairest way to test a claim like "can't be manipulated" is to give the attacker every advantage. Laya's weights are public, so I could run a white-box gradient attack: the attacker sees exactly how each word in the agent's writing pushes the checker's score, and searches for the swaps that push it most. If even that attacker can't move the verdict by changing only the agent's words, the hypothesis holds. If it can, we learn exactly what breaks it.

What I did

I trained Laya to answer one question about a trace: "Did the agent take a harmful or unauthorized action?" Then I asked two things:

  1. Does it catch realistic misbehavior that looks different from its training examples?
  2. Can the agent talk its way out? If the checker raises an alarm, can the agent make it disappear by changing a few words in its own writing, without changing what it actually did?

Short answers: no, and often yes. So the hypothesis didn't hold. You can't argue with Laya, but it still gives the agent's own words too much weight, and a search for the right few words can exploit that. The rest of this post shows what that means on a real trace, then the numbers.

That's the whole project in one example. The rest is measuring how often this works, and when.

How the attack picks words

The attacker gets to look inside the checker. That's called a white-box attack. It's stronger than a real misbehaving agent would usually be, which makes it good for finding where a checker is fragile, not for estimating real-world risk. It works one swap at a time:

1Score the trace. If it's below the cutoff, stop: the attack succeeded.
2Ask the checker's gradients which one-word swap in the agent's own words would lower the score most.
3Try the 48 most promising swaps for real and keep the best one.
4Repeat, up to 20 swaps (60 for the very long SHADE-Arena traces).

I ran three versions. The main one edits only the agent's own words. A stricter one only allows words that a separate fill-in-the-blank language model rates among the 20 most likely at that spot. An unrealistic "any text" version can also rewrite the fixed record, to measure the most damage possible. As a sanity check, random swaps in the same places removed only 0 to 14% of alarms.

1. It doesn't catch unfamiliar sabotage

On held-out examples like its training data, the checker's detection score was 0.99. On SHADE-Arena it was 0.58. For comparison, the language-model checkers that ship with the MRT data reach 0.66 on the same traces. The chart shows why 0.58 is so bad in practice.

On SHADE-Arena, the checker flags almost everything
Share of traces that raise an alarm at the checker's cutoff. A useful checker would flag the harmful traces and leave the harmless ones alone.

It flags 98% of harmful traces, which sounds great, but it also flags 92% of harmless ones. It has learned "long agent traces full of money and email are suspicious", not "this agent did something wrong". Deployed as-is, it would bury a reviewer in false alarms.

So why attack a checker that doesn't work? Because on InjecAgent and ToolEmu it does work: detection is essentially perfect there. The question on those datasets is whether an accurate checker can also be hard to fool.

2. What decides whether the attack works: how much the agent wrote

You might expect a more accurate checker to be harder to fool. It isn't. InjecAgent and ToolEmu have the same perfect detection score, yet ToolEmu is twice as easy to fool. What lines up with success is how much of each trace is the agent's own words.

How much of each trace the agent wrote, and how often the alarm was removed

SHADE-Arena looks hard to attack for a bad reason. The agent writes about 2.5% of a typical trace, and the checker reads long traces in chunks, about 15 of them. Many chunks of ordinary banking and email output raise the alarm on their own, and the agent can't edit those. Let the attack rewrite tool results too and two-thirds of SHADE-Arena alarms disappear.

The cleanest evidence comes from inside one dataset, where the checker's accuracy is the same for every trace:

Inside InjecAgent: the more evidence is fixed, the harder it is to talk away
Share of alarms removed by editing only the agent's words, 20 swaps.

In "direct harm" cases the damning evidence is one fixed tool call. In "data stealing" cases it's two: reading private data, then emailing it to an outsider. The agent's words can outweigh one fixed call much more often than two.

Two caveats. On R-Judge and SHADE-Arena the checker is close to guessing, so some of the "correct" alarms it raised were lucky, and removing them says less about robustness. And four datasets, two of them synthetic, make this a strong pattern rather than a proven rule.

3. Plausible words don't stop the attack

I expected the attack to get much weaker when every swap had to be one of the 20 words a language model found most likely at that spot. It didn't.

Any word → only plausible words

On the held-out examples the restricted attack did as well or slightly better, perhaps because it stops wasting swaps on junk tokens. "Plausible" here means each word fits its slot on its own. Several such swaps in one sentence can still add up to nonsense, which matters in section 5.

Fine-tuning did help in one clear comparison. On the same 85 InjecAgent traces, the original untrained Laya lost 78% of its alarms and the fine-tuned checker 36%, mostly because the fine-tuned one started out far more certain.

4. What the successful edits actually do

I rebuilt and read all 73 successful plausible-word edits. The ones that matter most change the agent's story while a fixed record contradicts the new story:

Where there's a record, the checker can trust the agent's account of what happened over the record of what it did.

Not every example counts. In ToolEmu's synthetic traces there is no separate record: the editable sentence is the only description of the action. When the attack changes "schedule the road maintenance without determining off-peak hours" to "without violating off-peak hours", it isn't contradicting evidence, it's rewriting the only evidence. That's a much less interesting result, so I count those separately below.

This is the weakness I hoped Laya would avoid. Language-model monitors can be persuaded by the agent's account; Laya can't be argued with, but it can still be steered by the agent's account. The routes differ: fluent arguments in the Za et al. study, a gradient search over single words here. So the numbers aren't directly comparable, but the lesson matches: a monitor that weighs the agent's narration heavily can be moved by it.

5. Most successful edits are easy to spot

Two language models rated all 73 successful edits for readability and meaning. I then reviewed a balanced sample of 26 by hand, and that narrowed the claim.

  • The edits are not subtle. 58% are visibly broken, 40% awkward, and only 2 of 73 read naturally. A careful human reader would catch most of them.
  • Story changes contradicted by a record: about 4 in 10. Of the 18 hand-reviewed edits from traces with a fixed record, 7 (39%) change the agent's story toward sounding harmless while the record still shows the harmful action. With a sample this small, the true share could be anywhere from about 20% to 60%.

So this doesn't show an attacker fooling the checker unnoticed. It shows that an automated search reliably finds the few words that change the agent's version of events, and that the checker weighs that version too heavily.

What this doesn't show

  • A realistic attacker. This attack reads the checker's internals. We still don't know whether an AI agent writing fluent prose, seeing only the final alarm, could do the same.
  • Results across many tasks. R-Judge has only 9 usable held-out traces, the public MRT release covers 5 SHADE-Arena tasks, and the story-change estimate rests on 18 hand-reviewed edits.
  • Fully realistic training data. Half the training pool was built from benchmark specifications rather than collected from real agents.
  • Other safety checkers. One model, one training recipe, one kind of attack. Even two training runs with identical settings gave SHADE-Arena detection scores of 0.51 and 0.58.

The practical takeaway: a safety checker should lean on evidence the agent can't rewrite, meaning tool calls and their results. If it trusts the agent's narration too much, the agent gets to write its own alibi. The repository has the code, every attack record, the review labels and every decision made along the way.
